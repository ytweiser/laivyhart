    The Rav's rule is up to 1,000 m beyond the last buildings. Set it to 0 to stay inside the edge. "Ignore anything closer than" lets each spot see past the buildings right next to it, since buildings do not count.

    How this works, and how far to trust it

    The three answers. Your spot is wherever the pin stands, for example a shul. The earliest inside the outline is the first place in the town where the sun is seen. The earliest beyond it allows the extra distance set above, which is where Rav Keller's vantage points lie for Ramat Shlomo.

    The edge. The page loads the buildings within 2.5 km of the pin from OpenStreetMap and joins every building to any other within 60 m, starting from the one nearest the pin. The outline is drawn around that group. It is only as good as the map: where OpenStreetMap is missing buildings, the edge will be wrong, so drag it. In a big city the buildings never stop, so there is no edge to find.

    The search. The page lays a grid of up to about 4,000 spots over the outline and the band around it, looks from each one toward where the sun comes up, then works out the best 20 inside and the best 20 overall exactly, with a skyline traced out to 80 km.

    The data. Heights come from worldwide terrain tiles of about 30 m (Mapzen Terrain Tiles on AWS, built mostly from SRTM). They describe the surface, so they partly include buildings and trees. To stand on the ground and not on a roof, each spot takes the lowest height within about 25 m of it, and anything closer than the distance set above is ignored. Air at sunrise comes from the Open-Meteo forecast when the date is within about two weeks. Places are found with OpenStreetMap Nominatim.

    How good is it. For Ramat Shlomo, where we have a careful bare-ground model, this kind of tracing was within about a minute on most days and two minutes on nine days out of ten. Elsewhere, treat it as an estimate to play with, not a luach.
