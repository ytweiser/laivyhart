How this was made, and how far to trust it

For every 30 m square in the box from 31.804 to 31.818 north and 35.208 to 35.226 east (about 3,300 squares), the real skyline was traced in every direction from 56° to 124° out to about 75 km, including Earth's curvature. For each day, the calculator found the moment the upper edge of the sun clears that skyline from each square, and kept the earliest.

The winning squares hop around by a few dozen meters from day to day, which is finer than the data can resolve. So the schedule uses 16 fixed points and keeps a point for as long as it is within 20 seconds of that day's best square.

Terrain is the Copernicus 30 m surface model. It includes buildings and trees, and its heights can be off by several meters. A few meters on a ridge 1.5 km away is roughly half a minute of sunrise. Treat times as good to about a minute and locations as good to about 50 m until someone checks them on the ground.

Because the surface model includes roofs, some points may be rooftops and not ground. Points A, C, J and M on the west side are the likeliest, since they sit toward the built streets. Open each point in Google Maps satellite view before relying on it.

The calculator uses this schedule by default: for each date it loads that day's point and its skyline, and it can then be adjusted for the morning's real temperature and pressure. Picking a different letter here and pressing the button sends that point to the calculator for that one day.

Border: the search went a little past Highway 21 on the east side. Point G appears to sit about 100 m beyond the road. That is accepted for now and will be redone if the Rav rules that the road is the border.

Air is fixed at 12°C and 926 hPa for the whole year. Real mornings differ by tens of seconds. The observer stands 1.7 m above the surface.

The reference point is the data square nearest 31.8125, 35.2225 (surface 811 m). In this model that square has slightly higher ground close by to the east and northeast, which is why it comes out later, most of all in summer. That needs a look on site.

Open question: a HeyWhatsThat reading from the reference point put the skyline at 111° almost exactly level, set by a ridge about 50 km away. This model instead finds a ridge about 1.7 km away standing about 0.6° above level in that direction. One photo of the eastern horizon from the hilltop would settle which is right.
