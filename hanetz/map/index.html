How this was made, and how far to trust it

For every 30 m square in the box from 31.804 to 31.818 north and 35.208 to 35.226 east (about 3,300 squares), the skyline was traced in every direction from 56° to 124° out to 80 km, including Earth's curvature. For each day, the calculator found the moment the upper edge of the sun clears that skyline from each square, and kept the earliest.

Buildings do not count. The terrain is FABDEM, a version of the Copernicus 30 m model with buildings and trees removed, so both the skyline and the observer are on bare ground. The observer's eye is 1.7 m above the ground.

The winning squares shift by a few dozen meters from day to day, which is finer than the data can resolve. So the schedule uses 8 fixed points and keeps a point for as long as it is within 20 seconds of that day's best square.

Because buildings were removed, a point can fall where a building actually stands. Point B, used in deep winter, is the likeliest case: the surface model showed about 10 m of structure there. Open each point in Google Maps satellite view before relying on it.

Removing buildings is itself an estimate, and ground heights can be off by a few meters. A few meters on a ridge 1.5 km away is roughly half a minute of sunrise. Treat times as good to about a minute and locations as good to about 50 m until someone checks them on the ground.

Air is fixed at 12°C and 926 hPa for the whole year. Real mornings differ by tens of seconds. The calculator adjusts for the morning's real temperature and pressure.

The calculator uses this schedule by default: for each date it loads that day's point and its skyline. Picking a different letter here and pressing the button sends that point to the calculator for that one day.

The reference point is 31.8125, 35.2225 (ground 812 m). On bare ground it is within half a minute of the best on 215 days of the year. It falls behind mostly in late autumn and winter, by up to nearly 4 minutes, and averages about 50 seconds behind over the year.

Border: all 8 points appear to lie on the Ramat Shlomo side of Highway 21, judging from a map screenshot. That has not been checked against a surveyed road line.

Open question: a HeyWhatsThat reading from the reference point put the skyline at 111° almost exactly level, set by a ridge about 50 km away. This model finds rising ground about 1.7 km away standing about 0.5° above level in that direction, even with buildings removed. One photo of the eastern horizon from the hilltop would settle which is right.
