What this version does and does not do

Solar position follows Meeus (Astronomical Algorithms) with nutation and aberration, checked against the PyEphem library to within a few seconds. Refraction uses the Saemundsson formula scaled by the pressure and temperature you enter, applied to the chosen point on the disk, not to the center.

The observation point and its skyline come from a 30 m bare-ground terrain model (FABDEM, which is the Copernicus model with buildings and trees removed), so buildings do not count as blocking the view. Each date uses the point on the hilltop east of Ramat Shlomo where the first sliver appears earliest. The map and schedule page explains the choice. The model still needs a check on the ground, so treat times as good to about a minute.

A spot picked on the map uses different data: worldwide elevation tiles of about 30 m (Mapzen Terrain Tiles on AWS, built mostly from SRTM), which describe the surface and so partly include buildings and trees. The eye is 1.7 m above that surface. Treat those times as estimates.

Not yet in this version: a temperature profile or inversion model (the "Refraction multiplier" field lets you explore that by hand). That is Phase 3.

Times are rounded to the second but the real uncertainty on any given morning is tens of seconds from the atmosphere alone, which is exactly what the later phases and the calibration log are for.

Place and time

Click anywhere on the map to stand there.

The coordinates fill themselves in with the point in the neighborhood where the first sliver is seen earliest on this date. Click the map, or type coordinates, to stand somewhere else: the page then traces the skyline for that spot from worldwide terrain data. That is an estimate. On our checked points it was within about a minute on most days and within two minutes on nine days out of ten, and it can be far off where a building stands right beside the spot. A spot you pick is for this visit only and is not remembered.

What counts as sunrise

Horizon

Skyline elevation is the angle of the ground the Sun actually rises behind (buildings do not count), measured from level. For a schedule point it is filled in from terrain data at the azimuth where the Sun appears; typing a number here replaces it. Observer height matters when the ground falls away toward the east; leave it at 0 if the skyline is above eye level. The last field only matters for a spot picked on the map: the worldwide data partly includes buildings, so if one beside the spot is blocking the view, enter 150 to look past it.

Atmosphere

Getting the latest forecast for the sunrise time.

Temperature and pressure fill themselves in from the latest forecast for the minute of sunrise, fetched each time the page opens. Station pressure is the pressure where you stand, not the sea-level value weather sites show. Jerusalem at 780 m sits near 925 hPa. The multiplier is a hand control for the inversion question: a strong morning inversion over the rift bends light more (try 1.2 to 1.5), dry warm air less.
